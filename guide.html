What makes a good RTS

Thirty years of real-time strategy, boiled down to eight ideas that designers, critics and players keep coming back to. Each one has a diagram, then the last part asks where Orbis fits and what you like.

01 · 02

Attention is the real resource

An RTS hands you more jobs than you can do at once. The good ones make choosing what to look at the skill, not how fast you can click.

What the sources agree on

  • Competing jobs are the core. Economy, production, research, attack and defence all want you at once (Brazie calls it spinning plates).
  • Busywork is not depth. Needing hundreds of actions a minute for basic tasks is the most common reason given for casual players leaving the genre.
  • Depth can come from elsewhere. Company of Heroes (squads) and Supreme Commander (scale) lean far less on clicking speed and are still deep (Casteel).
  • New games say it outright. Battle Aces aims to reward strategy (counters, timing) as much as execution; IMMORTAL: Gates of Pyre lets the AI handle pathing and fine control.
  • Help the player look in the right place: idle-worker alerts, objectives that pull attention, queues.

Reading the chart

  • Top left is where big-scale and economic games live: lots to decide, little to click. Supreme Commander and Offworld sit there.
  • Top right is the esports corner: deep, but you need the hands as well as the head. StarCraft II is the reference.
  • The middle is where squad games like Company of Heroes sit: micro matters, but in fewer, bigger decisions per fight.
  • Bottom right is the trap: clicking that doesn't buy any decisions. Automation, queues and smart defaults keep a design out of it.

With 100 000 units Orbis can't ask for per-unit micro, so it starts on the left of this chart whatever else we choose.

03

Fights you can win or lose by degrees

The player should steer how a fight goes through three levers, and be able to back out of a bad one. All-or-nothing fights make one mistake decide the game.

Binary or graded?

  • Graded losses: squads that lose one model at a time, morale, damaged vehicles, a retreat button (Company of Heroes, Dawn of War).
  • Hard to back out: slow units and little payoff for micro mean falling behind is often the end (Grey Goo).
  • Scale is its own nuance: in Supreme Commander each choice matters less, but there are many of them.

From Brandon Casteel's essay on control of tactical outcomes (Wayward Strategy, 2016).

04

Soft counters and mixed armies

Rock-paper-scissors is where balance starts, not where it ends. If a counter is total, skill, terrain and upgrades stop mattering and the game is about blobbing the right unit.

Give units identities beyond damage and health

RaiderSiege gun
  • Differ on many axes: range, speed, size, how they move, armour and damage types, abilities.
  • Cost is more than price: count build time and what you had to build first.
  • Kill speed is not linear: a unit losing members also loses damage, so double damage can mean losing one unit instead of five.
  • Mixed armies win: Total Annihilation is the example given, where a good attack used several unit types (Wardell).
05

The economy sets the pace

Resources cap how fast armies grow, act as speed bumps against early knockouts, and, placed in no-man's-land, drag players into each other.

How many resources?

Brad Wardell's rule of thumb: at least two kinds, no more than four. Offworld Trading Company is the exception that proves you can build a whole game out of a market.

Speed bumps

  • Upkeep: Warcraft III cuts gold income as your army grows, a brake on snowballing.
  • Gated tech: Age of Empires holds siege weapons back until later ages so nobody can turtle forever.
  • Rushes on purpose: StarCraft II allows dramatic early rushes, and pays for them with defence options.
  • Two jobs for two resources: one caps unit count (food, supply), one pays for buildings and defences.
06

Information is a weapon

Fog of war turns the map into a guessing game you can win by scouting. Every hidden threat needs a way to find it.

Tools for knowing

scoutswardswatch towers base modulesdetectioncloak

  • Pair every stealth with detection. StarCraft's cloak is a threat only until you build the answer, and the first time it appears teaches you to.
  • Stale information is still information. Explored ground shows the land and what was there, not what is there now.
  • Scouting is a decision. Units spent looking are units not fighting.
07

Pacing and comebacks

The two classic failures are games decided early and snowballs, where every win makes the next one easier. Tension needs swings.

Keeping it alive

  • Income penalties for big armies (upkeep) and resource speed bumps.
  • Room to retreat so a lost fight is a setback, not the end (section 03).
  • Objectives worth holding, like Age of Empires' sacred sites, that make the leader come out and fight.
  • Map events that move people, like rising lava, so nobody can sit still.
  • Campaigns in three acts: a quiet start, a complication, a final battle; new mechanics arrive as threats.

Most competitive designs aim for matches under half an hour; big-scale games such as Sins of a Solar Empire run far longer by design.

Content and accessibility keep a niche alive

Recent RTS launches stumbled less on multiplayer than on being only multiplayer. Players come for campaigns and bots, and stay for custom games and mods.

+

Playing at scale

The closest relatives of Orbis are the big-army games. They teach one lesson each: let the player zoom from a soldier to the whole map, and solve how they see a world they cannot see all at once.

Total Annihilation & Supreme Commander 1997 · 2007

  • Flow economy built for huge armies: income against spending, not a bank.
  • Strategic zoom from one unit to the whole map.
  • Interface and queueing built to command armies of ridiculous size.
  • Supreme Commander 2 fell back on genre norms and lost what made the first one different.

Planetary Annihilation 2014

  • Whole planets, many at once; armies of a size few engines can run.
  • Advanced queueing, several windows, and teams sharing one army.
  • Never found an interface that fit round planets: you only ever see part of the map.
  • Breaking an entrenched enemy was very hard if you had not expanded fast.
  • Thin tutorials; players were left to discover core systems themselves.
  • The Titans expansion fixed much of it by moving closer to Supreme Commander.

2025 was called the strongest RTS year in ages, without a breakout hit; Broken Arrow was holding almost 10,000 concurrent players a day.

Where Orbis stands

Orbis already has the hard technical parts the big-army games needed. What it doesn't have yet is the design that decides what playing it feels like.

From squads to armies: where could it sit?

Already built

  • A whole planet with a camera from the ground to space: strategic zoom is already the camera.
  • No unit cap: 100 000 units, 105 fps standing and 47 fps all walking.
  • A* with shared paths, formations that keep their shape, separation.
  • Fog of war in three states, buildings on flattened ground.
  • Climate, day and night, weather, clouds, sea and mist: a world worth fighting over.

Questions to answer together

  1. Squads or armies? Nursing a few units, or commanding tens of thousands?
  2. How do you see a sphere? Planetary Annihilation never solved it; zoom to icons is the strongest lead.
  3. Which economy? Stockpile, flow, or a market?
  4. How do fights end? A retreat, partial losses, morale?
  5. What does the planet itself do? Climate, weather, night and seasons as strategy?
  6. Who do you play against? A campaign, bots, friends, the planet?
