How many units?

Orbis can already run about 8,000 units in one fight. That doesn't mean it should. Ten thousand units on one screen are a few pixels each, no player can command them one by one, and a planet you take region by region keeps growing. This study looks at what other games do, what fits on a screen, what a player can control, and proposes a cap: a few dozen high-value colony units, managed one by one, against a planet that sends the masses.

01

What other games allow

Three families. Classic RTS caps each player at 100–200 so every unit matters. Big-army RTS allows 1,000–1,500 per player and leans on the hardware. Horde games give the huge numbers to the enemy, not the player.

Classic

100–200, every unit counts

StarCraft II, Age of Empires, Company of Heroes. Supply buildings raise the cap; bigger units cost more of it. Company of Heroes counts squads, so 100 pop is a few dozen things to command.

Big army

1,000–1,500, hardware is the limit

Supreme Commander, Beyond All Reason, Planetary Annihilation, Sins II. Players command groups and factories, not units. The fun is scale and logistics; the cost is slow late games.

Horde

The enemy brings the numbers

They Are Billions: up to 20,000 infected against a small, defended colony. The player's side stays small and precious. This is the closest to Orbis's "the planet fights back".

03

How many fit on one screen?

Your first question. Zoom out far enough to see 10,000 tanks and each one is a handful of pixels. Try it: this uses Orbis's own numbers (a tank is 3 m long, the camera's field of view is 46°).

Seen from straight above. A tilted camera sees more ground, and the far half of it smaller still.

–one unit's length on screen
–units that fit on the screen
–triangles per pixel it covers (over ~1 is wasted)
–triangles it actually needs at this size
The answer: to frame 10,000 tanks in formation (6 m apart) you zoom out to about 530 m, where each tank is 7–10 pixels long (1080p to 1440p). At that size the tank's 620 triangles are around 10–20 per pixel, and a 12–50 triangle version looks the same. Graphics cards also shade in 2×2-pixel blocks, so triangles smaller than a pixel waste work. You're right: far away, a unit needs a tiny fraction of its triangles.
04

Triangles a unit needs at each size

Detail should follow size on screen: full models up close, simpler ones further out, and the strategic icons from orbit. Atlas already has far LODs (1.11); Orbis's units don't use them yet.

64 px and up600the full model: turret, gun, tracks
24–64 px~200hull, turret and gun, no small parts
10–24 px~50a few boxes with the right outline and colour
4–10 px~12a box or a flat picture of the unit (an impostor)
under 4 pxiconthe strategic icon (the I key) takes over
The brute is the big one to fix: 9,926 triangles even at 10 pixels. A 300–600 triangle brute up close plus a far version would cut most of a big battle's triangles. It matters less than the CPU work per unit today, but more once the counts grow.
05

Can a player command that many?

Your second question. No: not one by one. A top StarCraft II player makes about 300 actions a minute (most players 50–100). Spread over the army, that's the attention each unit gets.

Command groups, not units

Squads of 3–5 (Company of Heroes, Total War): one order moves them all and they keep formation. 150 squads are 450 models but only 150 things to command.

Let them think a little

Stances already exist (hold, guard, hunt). Add rally points, patrols and "defend this region", so a front holds itself while you're elsewhere on the planet.

Command the planet, not the fight

From orbit you set where the fronts are; zoomed in you fight one battle. The strategic icons are the bridge between the two.

06

Why would you need that many?

Your third question, and your idea: you don't. The colony is few, strong and precious; the planet is many, fast and cheap. That's also the story: a small company on a world that treats it as an infection.

YouThe colony: quality

  • Fewer, bigger units that cost more, take longer to build and do much more: a heavy tank is worth five light ones, a walker ten.
  • Each loss hurts, so you protect and repair them (the turtle you are).
  • Smart: formations, stances, target choice. Paths cost per order, not per unit; it's the fighting and steering every frame that few units keep cheap.
  • Late tech gets stronger, not more numerous: experimental-class units, like Supreme Commander's, instead of a bigger cap.

The planetThe masses: quantity

  • Many, fast, fragile: runners die in a hit or two. The fun is mowing down a wave that almost reaches the wall.
  • Simple minds: a whole wave follows one flow field (Orbis already does this) and only steers when it is near something.
  • Cheap to draw: small, similar, far: ideal for low-detail models and shared animation.
  • The Given and the giants are the exception: few, huge and clever, the planet's own "big units".
07

The swarm: scale the threat, not the crowd

Your fourth idea. As the game gets harder the crowds stay the same size, or shrink, and bigger creatures come out. That keeps every fight affordable and makes each step up feel new rather than just bigger.

A budget, not a count

The planet has one pool of life to spend. A director keeps up to about 5,000 creatures active near you and holds the rest off-screen as numbers in each region, ready to wake.

Mob minds are cheap

One flow field per wave, steering only when close, target searches in rings (done in November): the cost per creature stays small however big the wave.

Set pieces can go huge

A scripted moment, a migration across the plains seen from far away, can still show 20,000, drawn as low-detail models or impostors, because nobody fights them one by one.

The short of it: Orbis already runs more creatures than World War Z shows. What World War Z has is the look up close and the way the horde behaves: it flows as one, piles up and climbs. That's what to take.

The camera is the difference

World War Z is played a few metres from the zombies, so each one needs a detailed body. At Orbis's play zoom (60–100 m) a 1.6 m runner is about 27–45 pixels tall on a 1440p screen: the crowd's motion matters more than each body. Close detail matters at ground zoom, for the few nearest the camera.

The idea to take: piles at the walls

It fits the tower-defence decision. Runners blocked at a wall heap up against it; once the heap reaches the top, they pour over. Walls buy time instead of being a hard stop, and every tower that thins the heap keeps it down. Only the creatures at the wall pay for it, a few hundred at most.

Bend Studio's open-world survival game (2019, PS4; on Unreal Engine 4). Its hordes aren't waves: they're places on the map with a routine.

The short of it: Days Gone makes a horde a thing in the world, with a home, a daily routine and a count that remembers what you did to it. That suits a planet you take region by region better than waves do.

Why it fits Orbis

The swarm's director already holds creatures as numbers in each region. Days Gone shows what to do with them: a horde with a home and a routine, on Orbis's own clock. Night is when they move, so lights, walls and towers matter most after dark.

The idea to take: hordes your units scout

It fits the expanding half of the towers-and-units decision. Units find where a region's horde sleeps and where it goes at night. Then you choose: build away from its path, lure it into your towers, or hit its nest by day.

08

A cap that fits a planet

Replaced on 10 November by 09: units now cost crews from your people and towers cost power. The relay points are dropped; the hard ceiling, upkeep, squads and the swarm's director carry over.

The proposal: command points that grow with the land you hold, up to a hard ceiling. Taking over the planet raises your cap instead of breaking it, and upkeep makes a big army a real cost.

1

Points from land

Like Company of Heroes 3's ports or StarCraft's depots, but tied to regions: expansion is how you grow.

2

Upkeep

The flow economy already has it. A rising cost per point is a soft cap well below the hard one.

3

Squads

A platoon is one order and one price; it looks like an army and costs like a unit.

4

The swarm's budget

The planet isn't capped by points but by a director: about 5,000 active near you.

09

Decided: towers hold, units go out

Your four ideas of 10 November, agreed. The colony becomes a tower-defence turtle that sends units out to expand. Its cap comes from your people rather than section 08's command points, and the view switches to radar sooner.

1Radar view nearer the ground

What changes

Icons fade in from about 200 m and are full by about 350 m. Once they are, the unit models are hidden: not drawn, not posed, out of the rays. The dots further out stay as they are.

What it saves

Drawing work: roughly 1–3 ms of a big battle seen from above (an estimate, to measure). Not the fighting or steering: those run whether anyone looks, and multiplayer needs the same simulation on every machine, whatever each camera sees.

What we keep

A toggle to see the models from any height, and set pieces that keep theirs. What's lost by default is the 300–500 m view of the swarm hitting your wall. Supreme Commander's strategic zoom shows players like the trade.

  • One pool of people. Homes and domes raise it (v1: "people are one number"). Army, economy and research all draw on it, so you can't field an army and staff every lab at once (the settlement page).
  • The relay command points are dropped. Taking a region still raises the cap, because it gives you room for more domes. Factories' build rate limits how fast an army comes.
  • A hard ceiling stays in the code (about 500 units) as a safety net that normal play never reaches.
  • Softening the spiral: a breach kills people, so you lose workers and army at once. Crews can escape their wrecks, and the planet eases off for a while after a big loss.

3Towers defend, units expand

TowersHold the wave

  • Much better value against the swarm than units, for what they cost.
  • Power is their limit, so generators are what the swarm goes for (v1: lose a generator and its turrets go dark).
  • Walls funnel the waves into killing zones; the swarm can break through them (Orbis already does), so a perfect maze is never safe.
  • Cheapest for the frame: a turret never paths or steers.
  • Each path has its own defences in the tech tree, its strongest line at the top of the branch.

UnitsGo out

  • They lose an even fight left to themselves: a wave beats an army of the same cost. Good micro (focus fire, pulling back, abilities) wins well above their mass (10).
  • They win by picking their fights: hitting nests between waves, escorting builders to new regions, plugging a breach, scouting.
  • They're how you grow: the rich ground is outside the wall (v1), and new regions mean new domes and a higher cap.
What keeps it from being passive. Waves that test different layers (burrowers under, flyers over, giants through). Things to do during a wave: overcharge turrets, light a fire moat, call in artillery. A nest you destroy stops its wave, so your units race a clock. And a planet that answers what you build most.

4The cost is per unit, not per path

Agreed: fewer, smarter units, for a slightly different reason than we thought. Paths are worked out once per order or wave, off the frame. What grows with the count is each unit's fighting, steering and drawing every frame (the table in 02). Towers are cheaper still.
10

Decided: crews, towers and the swarm's tiers

Decided on 10 November. The numbers are starting points to tune in play; 09 is updated where this changes it (plain walls, micro).

A colony of 20–60 crewed machines, each worth managing by hand, towers worth two to three times a unit behind good walls, and a swarm of thousands drawn in three tiers now that Atlas has crowds.

The feel, in your words (10 November): a classic RTS at the micro level with a macro feel. Few units, each of high value, managed one by one, fighting hordes with a feeling of massive effect.

Micro: few units that matter

Single units, not squads, with control groups. One or two abilities each. Crews earn ranks, and a crew that escapes its wreck keeps its rank: the machine is replaceable, the crew isn't. At 30–60 units even a 100-actions-a-minute player gives each one a command every minute or so (05).

Massive effect: each shot kills many

A late army of 60 against about 5,000 creatures is 80 to a unit, so the weapons have to mow: splash, flame, chain lightning, artillery. Creatures die in bursts and pile up, fire chars the ground, and Orbis's budgeted death effects keep it cheap.

Macro: the planet around it

Domes, the power grid and tower lines run the economy and hold the waves while your attention is on the units. Region by region, the planet is the macro game.

09 is updated to match: units still lose an even fight left to themselves, but good micro (focus fire, pulling back, abilities) wins well above their mass. That's the skill on the unit side, as the wall layout is on the tower side.

2How much better towers are

Power is the tower cap

A generator gives 100 power. A light tower draws 10, a heavy one 25, a shield 40. Four to six generators keep a decent line lit, so they're what the swarm goes for.

Plain walls cost mass only

A wall doesn't need power to stand, and drawing power would make long walls pointless. Shield walls and gates draw power. This changes 09's table.

What towers don't get

No people, so a tower line doesn't take soldiers from expanding. They don't move or pack up, and repairs need a builder nearby: a job for builders during waves.

3Machines, crewed by people

No humanoid units: every unit is a machine with a crew inside. The other two options are kept below for reference.

chosenCrewed machines

People stay the unit cap (09). They're seen only as the domes' life, or not at all. Metal against creature reads at a glance, with fewer animations to make.

Power armour or small mechs

Humanoid in shape, built of rigid parts, so they animate as cheaply as the walker. Keeps a soldier role without skinned humans.

Fully robotic, no crews

Undoes the people cap, which would need a new source (control range, a drone limit). Not recommended: crews tie the army to the settlement, which is what makes a breach hurt.

Not a performance question any more. With Atlas's bone parts (below), a humanoid costs about what a rigid walker does, shadows kept. Machines-only is now a choice about the look and the art work.

4The swarm in three tiers

Atlas's crowds (armies step 4) are merged into Atlas develop at e7b4b2a, for 1.17; not released yet.

About 5,000 near you

Crowd copies cost almost no memory each, so the director's figure looks reachable. To measure: the unit lab's crowd runs at 1,000 and 5,000.

The radar band helps

Past about 350 m the models go anyway, so the creatures only need to look right in the first few hundred metres.

The colony doesn't change

Its machines are rigid parts posed on the GPU already, and 40–60 of them is cheap.
