Orbis · environment round 1 · towards concept v2

A world that isn't Earth

How films and games make an alien world feel alien, and what Orbis takes from it. The lesson is the same everywhere: start from real ground, then change the life, the liquids and the light, and tie it all to one idea. Orbis already has that idea: the substance. Near the equator the planet looks almost like home. Toward the poles the substance rises, and the world gets stranger, richer and angrier. Overhead, two moons and a broken moon's ring make the sky the planet's calendar.

01

What other worlds do

Ten principles from films and games. We take the principle, never the design: no sandworm, no Pandora. Each card ends with what it means for Orbis.

1 · Start from real ground, then change one thing

Prometheus shot its alien world in Iceland, Dune's Arrakis is real desert in Jordan and Abu Dhabi, and Starship Troopers' Klendathu is real badlands. Earth already has alien places: Ethiopia's Dallol gets its yellows and greens from sulphur and salt. Subnautica's team found real reefs already look alien, so they pushed theirs further.

For Orbis: keep the photo-scanned rock and sand (stage 8). Change the life, the liquids and the light.

2 · The life is the strange part, not the rock

Annihilation's designers built the Shimmer's look on refraction and mutation: several species growing from one stem, trees in human shapes, crystal trees on a beach. Pandora's plants glow and react to touch. Science agrees colour can change: under a different sun, plants could be black or purple.

For Orbis: the native plants aren't green (section 05).

3 · One idea explains the whole world

On Arrakis the lack of water, the spice and the giants are one cycle. Nausicaä's toxic jungle turns out to be cleaning poisoned soil. On Metroid Prime's Tallon IV a corruption spreads from one crater, and the regions show how far it got.

For Orbis: the substance. How close it lies to the surface shapes every biome, and the goo is the substance as a liquid (sections 02 and 04).

4 · The world is one living system

Pandora's life is one network. Warhammer 40,000's death worlds make every plant a predator: the world itself is the enemy, not what lives on it.

For Orbis: v1 has this already (everything is the immune system). So biomes aren't scenery: they're how the planet's body is laid out, and they fight back in their own ways.

5 · Hazards with rules you can learn

On Arrakis you learn how to cross open sand without calling the giant. In Subnautica depth is danger, and you always know how deep you are. Each of Returnal's biomes on Atropos has its own enemies and hazards.

For Orbis: every hazard has a tell (you see or hear it coming) and a counter. Noise draws giants (v1).

6 · Ground that belongs to the enemy

StarCraft's creep is living ground: the Zerg move faster on it, nobody else can build on it, and it spreads. In They Are Billions the map type sets how hard and how rich a game is.

For Orbis: the creeping forest and the bloom are ground that spreads. Biome borders move, and they're part of the fight.

7 · Night is a second world

Pandora glows at night; Subnautica's deep water is dark and full of lights. The same place becomes somewhere else.

For Orbis: by day a strange wilderness; by night the core's cold teal light shows in the plants, the goo and the Given (lore page: they glow with it). v1's long nights are when the planet is strongest.

8 · One impossible thing, at scale

Pandora's floating mountains; the vast built shapes in Prometheus' natural valley; Klendathu's bugs firing into orbit. Supreme Commander and Planetary Annihilation make scale readable by zooming from one unit out to the whole planet.

For Orbis: giants and megaflora taller than any building, and every biome has one thing you can see from orbit.

9 · Stranger the deeper you go

No Man's Sky's art director said worlds grow more unsettling toward the galaxy's centre. Returnal's biomes run in order. Metroid Prime puts frozen drifts next to magma caverns. Planetary Annihilation's planet types (desert, ice, lava, metal) each change what you can mine.

For Orbis: the equator is almost Earth; the poles are the alien heart. v1 already pushes you from the equator toward a pole.

10 · Every place has a palette

No Man's Sky took its colours from old sci-fi book covers: each planet has a personality. Returnal is dark with one glowing accent, beauty and aggression together.

For Orbis: each biome has its own palette and one accent. The core's teal is the planet's signature, in every biome.
What to avoid: Earth with a filter. The same grass and pines, tinted purple. Supreme Commander's maps are Earth's biomes, which suits its war but isn't alien. The fix is to change shapes and behaviour, not only the colour: plants that move, liquids that set into stone, ground that heaves.
02

The biomes, equator to pole

Two of Earth's rules plus one of ours. Orbis's climate already sets the ground by temperature (latitude and height) and moisture. Add a third: the substance, how close the core's energy lies to the surface. It's low at the equator and rises toward the poles, so the world gets stranger the further you push.

The Mire

equator · hot · wet · low

Flat, warm swamp: black water between firm hummocks, trees standing on their roots, mist every dawn. The richest life on the planet.

From orbit: a dark belt under morning mist.

The Scrub

tropics · hot · dry seasons

Open rust-red grassland with groves of spore towers standing like chimneys. Firm, open ground: where you land and build first.

From orbit: the towers' plumes of mist.

The Deep Desert

dry band · hot days, cold nights

Dunes, salt pans and rust brine springs. Almost nothing grows. Glass storms, and the giants that swim under the sand (section 08).

From orbit: the giants' wakes, and storm walls.

The Creeping Forest

temperate · wet · seasons

Tall trunks under wine-red and black-violet sheet canopies. The megaflora's home: the forest moves toward you and swallows walls (v1).

From orbit: a dark-red belt whose edge shifts.

The Pale

cold · long seasonal nights

Grey-white lichen, bone-coloured trees, freezing fog. The substance is close enough that the frost glitters with it. Ruins start to appear.

From orbit: white with a glitter.

The Glass

pole · night for a mission

Ice and crystal grown together, so you can't tell which is which. The great pits, the aurora, the long dark. The endgame.

From orbit: the aurora (v1: the core breaking through).

The Spines

high ground · any latitude

Rock ridges with crystal outcrops, wind and lightning. Height counts as cold, so a high Spine near the equator wears a little Pale and Glass on top.

From orbit: glittering ridge lines.

Goo basins

low ground · more toward the poles

Where the substance wells up as a liquid and pools: warm, thick, glowing faintly. Shores crusted with crystal (section 04).

From orbit: teal glow at night.

The Hollows

round every pit

Wet, dark ground in cold teal light, pale growth like fungus, mist pouring out (the lore page's pits). The Given's ground.

From orbit: a column of mist.

How they blend

  • Wide edges with their own character. Not a colour fade but a place: the reed margin floods in the wet season, the buried wood is dead forest under dunes, the frost-glass line is where crystal starts to grow out of frost.
  • Height moves you toward the pole. The climate already cools with height; mountains anywhere get the colder biomes on top.
  • Rich seams bring a little pole with them. Wherever the substance is close, even near the equator, you see glass and teal. That's how you spot good ground, and the danger that comes with it.

Borders move my pick

  • The forest creeps into the Scrub while a region is quiet, and into your base if you let it.
  • The desert grows behind a giant's wake, burying the forest edge.
  • The Hollows widen as a pit grows (lore page: pits left alone get wider).
  • You push back: fire, clearing, draining, terraforming. Over a campaign the map you hold looks different from the one you landed on.
03

Swamps: the Mire

Few RTS games have real swamps, so this is a chance to make one that plays differently. The rule of the Mire: the planet's creatures move freely, your heavy army doesn't. You fight along chains of firm ground, and the Given come up out of the water.

Movement

  • Black water (shallow, most of it): infantry 60%, tracked vehicles 50%, wheeled can't enter, legged and hover units full speed.
  • Deep channels: hover and amphibious only, plus the Given, who swim (they were made in the wet dark).
  • Hummocks: firm islands at full speed. A chain of them is a road, and a natural choke point.
  • The planet's own beasts and Given move at full speed. In the Mire they're faster than you.

Building my pick: all three

  • On hummocks: normal cost. Few and far apart: natural strongpoints.
  • On pilings: anywhere shallow, at +50% cost and time. Walls on pilings are weaker.
  • Drain it: engineers drain a patch into firm ground (terraforming). Slow, raises irritation, and the Mire creeps back unless a pump keeps running. Lose the power and the base floods: the power grid matters twice here.

Visibility

  • Mist at dawn and through the night: sight halved.
  • Reeds hide anything in them until it's close, for both sides. The Given use them; so can you.
  • Sound carries over water: sensors hear a wave from further off (lore page: sound before sight).
  • Orbit sees little: the mist blocks the uplink (v1) until you clear it.

Hazards, and chances

  • Rot gas pools under the water. Fire or a big explosion sets it off: a sheet of fire across the water. Light it under a wave and it's a weapon; under your base it's a disaster.
  • Drowners: snare plants under the surface that grab infantry and pull them down.
  • Flooded pits: the Mire's pits are under water, so the Given come up where you can't see a nest.
  • Lightning jumps further on wet ground (weapons page): tesla towers are at their best here, and so is the planet's own lightning. Fire is weak, apart from the gas.

Why go into the Mire

  • Wildlife: the richest specimens on the planet. Every kind of beast passes through.
  • Seams that grow back: the substance crystallises fast in standing water. Small seams, but they regrow.
  • Ancients: drowned ruins. The first colony landed by water, and the Mire took it.
  • A natural moat: a base on a hummock ring is hard to reach for heavy enemies (and the rival).
04

No real oceans: goo seas and glass rivers

No open sea. Low ground holds patches of liquid instead, and the main one is the substance itself. The proposal: the substance has a life cycle. It rises from the core as a liquid, flows downhill, and sets into crystal as it cools. The seams in the rock are old goo.

Amy pick

No ocean at all

Goo seas in basins, glass rivers, the Mire's black water and a few smaller liquids. Nothing you could sail. Every body of liquid is a place with a purpose.

Good: unmistakably not Earth; no naval game to build.

B

One small real sea, plus goo

A shallow water sea near the equator (the landing coast), goo everywhere else.

Good: a familiar start that turns stranger. Less good: it's the Earth part.

C

A great goo sea at each pole

As A, plus a frozen-over goo sea at each pole around the great pits, crusted and cracking.

Good: a huge image from orbit. Less good: big poles are hard to build on.

Amy pick

Native life isn't green

Rust, wine-red, black-violet and teal. The only green on the planet is what you bring: your crops and gardens. From orbit, your settlements are the green spots: the infection, seen from the planet's side.

Good: instantly alien with real rock and sky. On the Stabilise path the green and the native colours mix.

B

Green, with alien shapes

Familiar colours; the strangeness is in the shapes (stilt roots, sheets, towers) and the behaviour.

Good: easy to read as "plants". Less good: close to Earth with a filter.

C

Greener at the equator

Near-Earth greens where you land, turning rust, red, then white and glass toward the pole.

Good: the "stranger the further you go" idea in colour. Less good: a gentler first look.

Plants are part of the fight. Fire is the answer to megaflora (weapons page), and Orbis already burns forests (stage 16: trees catch, fire runs through a wood and stops at gaps). Sap pools and rot gas give fire more to work with; rain and the Mire take it away.
06

Storms and weather

Weather already moves over Orbis: clouds, fronts, rain, snow, fog and wind. Each biome gets its own storm, and the big ones ride the agitation: an angry region brews worse weather, and when the heart crosses a threshold (v1's planet-wide surges), every biome's storm comes at once.

Warning, so storms are fair

  • From orbit you watch them build: the cloud systems are already there.
  • A forecast on the clock: "glass storm in four minutes".
  • On the ground: the sky changes colour, the wind turns, dust lifts at the horizon.
  • The point (principle 5): you can always prepare. Storms punish bases that weren't ready, not bad luck.

Storms and weapons (weapons page)

  • Rain puts out fire and makes lightning jump further.
  • Mist, dust and glass storms cut laser range; guns and explosives don't care.
  • Aurora storms hurt everything that runs on the grid, so a material base with stockpiled mass rides them out better than an energy base.
07

The sky: moons and close planets

The sky is where scale lives, and Orbis's camera goes from the ground to space, so whatever hangs there has to work from both. The proposal: the sky is the planet's calendar. Eclipses, tides and the worst nights all show up in it before they arrive.

What other skies do

  • A clock you can see. Majora's Mask's moon falls over three days, and you watch it come. Pitch Black's eclipse comes every 22 years, and its creatures come out with the dark.
  • One huge thing that never leaves. Pandora hangs beside a gas giant; Subnautica's world shares its sky with a planet that never moves; Bloodborne's moon tells you it's the late game.
  • A sky that hurts. In Outer Wilds, Brittle Hollow sits under a volcanic moon that bombards it while its own surface crumbles away.
  • One image says "not Earth". Tatooine's two suns need no explaining.
For Orbis: the sky warns you, tells you where you are on the planet, and now and then hurts.

Real geometry sells it

  • A ring lies over the equator. From the equator you see it edge-on, a thin line straight overhead. Further out it's a wide arc toward the equator. Near the poles it sinks below the horizon.
  • A moon locked face to face (a twin planet) never moves in the sky. Half the planet always sees it; the other half never does.
  • Moons pull tides, and two moons in line pull hardest.
For Orbis: the sky changes as you push from the equator to the pole, so it doubles as a map. At the pole the ring is gone and the aurora takes its place.
Amy pick

Two moons

A big slow moon, about four times the width of ours, with phases over several days. It pulls the goo tides and now and then crosses the sun. And a small fast moon, a bright point crossing the sky twice a night: a clock you can count to dawn.

Good: tides, eclipses and a calendar at the lowest cost. Readable at a glance.

Bmy pick, added to A

A broken moon: the ring

A ring of glittering debris over the equator, all that's left of a third moon. It glitters teal because it's full of the substance: the heart broke it long ago. Pieces still fall as meteors, and each one leaves fresh crystal where it lands.

Good: the shot from orbit, a lore warning (this is what the heart can do), and meteor events.

C

A twin planet

A sister world locked face to face with Orbis, fixed in one place in the sky. On the near side it hangs there forever and nights are never fully dark (twin-light). On the far side it's never seen and the nights are black.

Good: the geography decides the night: the far side is where the Given are strongest. Less good: one huge image, the same everywhere on the near side.

D

Orbis is a giant's moon

A banded giant planet fills a quarter of the sky, and in some regions it eclipses the sun every day.

Good: the biggest image of all. Less good: close to Pandora's sky, so it would need a strong look of its own; daily eclipses wear thin.

Colour and light

  • One sun (my pick), a touch whiter than ours. A second, distant sun would be a bright star by day and give a second dusk; two real suns mean two shadows, which is engine work.
  • The day sky can be Earth blue, or slightly wrong (pale teal, amber haze). Real ground with a slightly wrong sky reads alien without trying hard.
  • Nights near the equator are lit by the ring after dusk; nights at the poles are truly dark, matching v1's darkest nights at the poles.

Ties to the story

  • The ring is what's left of the heart's last great surge. The ruins' murals show a sky without it.
  • Your station is a moving star: the uplink (v1's orbital support). Storms and mist hide it.
  • The rival shows as a new light that grows over several missions: a countdown you can see.
  • The ancients' home could be a faint red star the ruins point at, where the beacon calls (lore page, section 03).

Can Orbis draw it?

  • Today: one sun, the stars turning with the planet, the atmosphere, and moonlight as a second light. The moon itself isn't drawn.
  • Game work: goo tides (move the water level; all goo shares it), meteors (an effect, a crater, a seam), dimming the sun and sky in an eclipse (both are settings).
  • Atlas work: moons and planets drawn at game scale, with phases, so they're right from the ground and from orbit; a ring that's lit, shadowed and casts a shadow on the land; an eclipse's black disc; more than one moon's light.
08

The desert giant

You imagined great burrowing worms. To keep it ours and not a sandworm, the proposal is a different body with a different reason: a giant that swims under the sand and feeds on the substance. It comes for the same seams you mine.

Amy pick

Ploughs

Broad, flat, many-paddled giants that swim just under the sand, grinding crystal seams. Their backs are ridged with crystal plates, so all you see is a dune moving against the wind with plates breaking the surface. They don't swallow anything: they heave the ground.

Good: the danger is to your base and the land itself, not a mouth; ties straight into the substance; cheap to draw (section 10).

B

Spine-sleepers

A giant that lies still for years as a ridge of crystal spines: a landmark you'd build next to. When the region's irritation peaks it wakes, drags itself out of the ground and walks off, leaving a trench.

Good: a betrayal you can see coming if you watch the meter. Less good: rare by design.

C

Storm-walkers

Tall, stilt-legged giants that walk inside the glass storms: the storm is their dust. In the murk you see only legs. When the storm clears, the giant has gone and so has whatever stood in its path.

Good: storms and giants become one threat. Less good: not burrowing.

How Ploughs play

  • They feed on seams. Rich ground draws them, and so does the noise of your extractors and drills (v1: giants follow noise and vibration). Your mine is eating their food.
  • You see them coming: a dune moving against the wind, a rumble, sand sliding off the plates. Ground sensors (the slice's sensors) track them under the sand.
  • They heave the ground. On their path buildings crack and topple, walls break, and a ridge rises where there wasn't one. The map is changed.
  • The wake is rich: churned sand with crystal showing, and a flash bloom that heals the swarm. Mine it fast, before the next wave.

What you can do about one

  • Lure it: thumpers away from your base (wildlife's lures, weapons page).
  • Dig in deep: hardened foundations on salt pans and bedrock ride out a pass.
  • Kill it: a giant fight. Armoured class: plasma, acid, the substance's own weapons.
  • Let it pass and rebuild on the new ridge, with a rich wake to mine.
  • Guide it (wildlife, late): on the Stabilise path a Plough can be steered, ploughing the ground for you.

Names to try: Ploughs, sand-rays, tillers.
