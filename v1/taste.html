Orbis concept · round 1 read back

A builder who wants to feel safe first, then win big

Every game you loved rewards building up and then using what you built. The one you disliked doesn't let you build at all. This page reads your answers back, lays out three directions for Orbis, and ends with the next round of questions.

The four loudest answers

  • Base building 100/100: big sprawling bases are not optional.
  • Sci-fi or fantasy 100/100: no historical or grounded settings.
  • Single-player 9/100: campaign and AI, not ladders.
  • Factions 72/100: sides that play differently, Generals style.

And one tension to design around

Scale leans to huge armies (64) but control leans to micro (38). The way games square that: a few units you really care about (heroes, a Commander) leading many you don't.

Your own words: build defences and the economy, then stage an attack. Losing to an instant rush is what you can't stand. "I guess I'm a turtle."

03

Six patterns

Build up, then strike

The payoff is using what you built: nukes, Total Annihilation's extra tech tiers, Generals' superweapons, Supreme Commander's experimentals.

Safe early, dangerous late

No knockout in minute three. Threats that grow, and that you can see coming.

Something big and hostile

Sandworms, They Are Billions' swarms, whole planets. The world can be the enemy.

Builder first, commander second

Place every wall and turret yourself, keep the economy simple, and feel your armies through a few key units.

Sci-fi, distinct sides

Futuristic settings and factions that play differently, like Generals or Tempest Rising.

Why Total War missed

No base building, and the strategy happens on a separate map from the battles. Everything you loved has you building where you fight.

04

The turtle curve

Your fun lives where your power grows faster than the threat, with a few close calls, until you have enough to go and end it.

How games keep a turtle safe early

  • A landing period: no attacks for the first few minutes.
  • Small, announced first waves that teach you where to build walls.
  • Cheap, strong early defence: holding should cost less than attacking.
  • Warning time: scouting, sensors and orbital scans show what's coming.
  • AI that builds up instead of rushing, with a difficulty setting for how soon it pushes.

The danger comes later and bigger: escalating waves, a final wave, rival factions arriving. That keeps the close calls without the cheap losses.

05

Where your taste meets Orbis

The hard parts are already built, and they are the parts your taste needs most.

100 000 units

Swarms bigger than They Are Billions', already measured at 47 fps with all of them walking.

A living planet

Climate, deserts, weather, clouds, day and night: the world can be the enemy, worms and all.

Ground to space

Watch the nuke land up close, or the orbital strike from above. Strategic zoom for free.

06

Three directions

Three ways to turn that into a game. They can be combined; the next section shows how.

AThe planet is the enemy

They Are Billions × Supreme Commander on a sphere. Land on a hostile world and build out; its life answers with growing swarms, megafauna roam the deserts, storms and night make it worse. Climb the tiers to planet-scale weapons.

BA war for one planet

Dune II × Planetary Annihilation. Very different factions fight over one globe; the campaign takes it region by region. AI that builds up rather than rushes, plus landing periods and strong early defence.

CEscalation war

Total Annihilation / Supreme Commander / Generals skirmish against AI. Climb tiers to giant experimentals, orbital weapons and artillery that fires over the curve of the planet.

07

The recommendation: all three, in layers

A as the core, C as the spine that runs through every match, B as the layer that arrives later. Nobody has made it: They Are Billions is flat, and Planetary Annihilation has no living world fighting back.
